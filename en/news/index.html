Middle East key stories, from regional media and YouTube reports

  1. 1
    Iran says it will use “all available means” against the US blockade; Trump says new strikes after the new year if there is no deal
    • On the 23rd Iran's foreign ministry called the US blockade of Iranian ports “military aggression” and said it would use every available means to counter it (Al Jazeera).
    • Foreign Minister Araghchi said the blockade “cannot be sustained” and that a deal to reopen the strait and a nuclear deal are separate issues. Iran says the Strait of Hormuz could be open within two days if the US halts hostilities and lifts the blockade and sanctions.
    • President Trump was reported as saying on the 22nd that he would order new strikes after the new year if no deal is reached. Bahrain said on the 22nd that it had intercepted a number of aerial attacks from Iran; falling debris caused minor damage but nobody was hurt. It said two people were wounded the day before in a strike on a civilian industrial site.
    Why it matters: talks are stalled while threats and attacks continue. The Strait of Hormuz is the route for much of the world's crude oil.
    Sources: Al Jazeera / CBS News / Axios / Middle East Monitor / Yahoo News (AFP) / Bangkok Post (Bahrain) / YouTube (Middle East Eye) / YouTube (Al Jazeera English)
  2. 2
    Yemen: strike hits a telecom building in the port city of Hodeidah; Houthis say 17 people were killed
    • According to Al Masirah, the TV channel of the Houthis (a Yemeni armed group close to Iran), a Saudi strike hit a state telecom building in Hodeidah in the west. The Houthis first reported four deaths, then 10, then 17. The Saudi-led coalition has not responded, and the details have not been independently confirmed.
    • On the 20th the coalition struck Hodeidah port, saying it had been used to smuggle weapons and ballistic missiles.
    • Houthi media released video of what they say are weapons depots seized near the Red Sea town of Mocha after government forces withdrew (a Houthi claim that has not been verified).
    Why it matters: the exchange of attacks goes on, and people living in a Red Sea port city are caught in it.
    Sources: The New Arab / Politis / Kurdistan24 / Daily Sun / The National (Nov 20) / YouTube (Middle East Eye)
  3. 3
    West Bank: attacks on Palestinian medics have surged, an Israeli rights group reportsLittle covered in Japan
    • On the 23rd Physicians for Human Rights Israel published a report saying Palestinian emergency medical workers in the West Bank face assaults, threats and obstruction from soldiers and settlers.
    • Attacks on healthcare rose from 93 in 2022 to 422 in 2024 (Al Jazeera describes this as a 350% surge). The report is based on interviews with 25 paramedics, medics and volunteers in the Nablus area.
    • The report's author describes a “dangerous escalation” since October 7, 2023.
    Why it matters: when the people who go to help the wounded are not safe, lives that could be saved are lost.
    Sources: Yahoo News (Al Jazeera) / Arab News / Arab News PK / YouTube (Al Jazeera English) / YouTube (Al Jazeera English, a paramedic)
  4. 4
    Gaza: a UN agency worker is killed in a strike while working on water supplyLittle covered in Japan
    • Adel Al-Helu, an employee of the UN agency for Palestinian refugees (UNRWA), was killed on the 23rd in an Israeli strike on the Jabalia refugee camp in northern Gaza (Quds News Network).
    • An UNRWA spokesperson said the strike hit a street near a water well where staff were carrying out routine water-supply work. Some reports say three other staff members were wounded.
    Why it matters: a ceasefire is supposed to be in place, yet even those who bring water to people are losing their lives.
    Sources: Quds News Network / YouTube (Al Jazeera English)
  5. 5
    “Gaza has become a toxic wasteland,” says a report by the aid group OxfamLittle covered in Japan
    • Oxfam and the Conflict and Environment Observatory say the war has left Gaza a “toxic wasteland” where human survival will be under threat for decades.
    • Signs of possible contamination were found across more than 99% of the territory. The UN estimates that about 61 million tonnes of debris now cover Gaza.
    • Because Israel does not allow assessments on the ground, the analysis relies on satellite imagery and remote sensing.
    Why it matters: even when the fighting stops, lives cannot be rebuilt while the soil and water remain polluted.
    Sources: The New Arab / The Journal / Quds News Network / YouTube (TRT World)
  6. 6
    Israel: coalition talks end without a deal; the deadline is December 6
    • Prime Minister Netanyahu's Likud and former army chief Eisenkot's Yashar party met on the 22nd but ended without agreement. No date has been set for the next meeting.
    • The two sides are split over who would be prime minister and whether the far-right politician Ben-Gvir would sit in the government.
    • Eisenkot has until December 6 to form a government, with a possible 14-day extension. Dozens of demonstrators gathered outside his home, urging him to partner with the Arab-led parties rather than Netanyahu.
    Why it matters: who ends up in power will shape how Israel deals with Gaza, the West Bank and Iran.
    Sources: Al Jazeera / Times of Israel / L'Orient Today / Arab News Japan / Dawn / Saudi Gazette / YouTube (TRT World)

    • The UN force UNIFIL says that in October it counted about 2,840 Israeli artillery rounds, 90 air strikes and 870 airspace violations in the south, which it says violate UN Security Council Resolution 1701.
    • UNIFIL's mandate runs until the end of 2026, and it is handing over positions in the south to the Lebanese army.
    • According to Lebanon's health ministry, Israeli strikes since March 2 have killed at least 4,390 people and displaced more than one million.
    Why it matters: once the UN force that keeps watch is gone, the question is who will protect people in the south.
    Sources: Arab News PK / SANA / QNC News

    • In late October Iraq's prime minister also visited Damascus; the two sides pledged closer security cooperation and discussed oil pipelines linking Iraqi and Syrian ports (Arab News).
    Why it matters: after a long war, Syria is trying to rebuild its ties with its neighbours and with Europe.
    Sources: SANA / Arab News (October)
  9. 9
    Commentary by Kazuyasu Ishida (Ekkyo 3.0): the call for a “national unity government” is a trap set by NetanyahuLittle covered in Japan
    • According to Ishida, the Likud–Yashar talks on the 22nd lasted more than four hours but decided nothing. There are two points neither side will give up: who becomes prime minister (Eisenkot says he will serve the full four years, while Likud proposes rotating after two years each), and whether the party of the far-right politician Ben-Gvir joins the government.
    • Ishida says there are three aims behind Netanyahu's call for “one government for all because the country is in danger”: to stay on as prime minister, to protect himself from his own trial, which is on hold while he is in office, and to prevent a state commission of inquiry into October 7, 2023, and appointments such as the attorney general, from going against him. Eisenkot is quoted as saying, “This is a trap, and it will not work on me.”
    • Points that Ishida says are “hardly reported in Japan”: until a new government is formed, Netanyahu, who lost the election, remains caretaker prime minister and is in a position to direct the military. Israel's Channel 12 reported that two days before the election the army chief of staff resisted an instruction for an operation involving Iran and asked for signed legal approval (the government denies this). And on November 17 a member of Eisenkot's party was elected speaker of parliament by 66 votes to 52.
    • If the talks collapse, Ishida reports, Eisenkot intends to seek a confidence vote for a minority government by the December 6 deadline. If the Arab-led parties and the ultra-Orthodox Jewish parties abstain, it could pass even without a majority of 61 seats.
    Why it matters: Ishida says that who forms the next government will decide whether Iran is attacked again, what happens to the Strait of Hormuz, and what happens to the oil that reaches Japan.
    Sources: YouTube (越境3.0チャンネル)

World top 5 important stories that get little airtime on Japanese TV

  1. 1
    Belgium: a national strike against austerity grounds all flightsLittle covered in Japan
    • On the 23rd the three main trade unions held a national strike against the government's austerity plans. Air traffic controllers joined in, and Brussels Airport cancelled all flights from 7 a.m. to 9 p.m. The airport said 429 flights and about 59,500 passengers were affected. Charleroi airport was also shut for the day, and trains and buses were disrupted.
    • Tens of thousands of people marched in Brussels. TRT World and Al Jazeera report clashes between police and protesters.
    • The government wants to save about 10 billion euros; pensions and the system that raises pay in line with prices are the main issues. Prime Minister De Wever has said his coalition will collapse unless it agrees a budget by the end of the year.
    Why it matters: after France, calls to protect ordinary people's living standards are growing louder in Europe.
    Sources: VRT / France 24 / Al Jazeera / Yahoo News / Saudi Gazette / YouTube (TRT World) / YouTube (Al Jazeera English)
  2. 2
    UK formally recognises the genocide against the Rohingya and sanctions Myanmar military officersLittle covered in Japan
    • On the 23rd the UK government said it formally recognises the genocide committed against the Rohingya, a minority in Myanmar. The move follows the International Court of Justice ruling of the 19th.
    • It sanctioned five senior Myanmar military officers and expelled Myanmar's defence attaché. It also plans legislation to stop the export of aviation fuel to Myanmar, and announced 5 million pounds in humanitarian aid and up to 3 million pounds to support justice for survivors.
    • The UK is one of 14 countries that acknowledged the court's ruling in a joint statement.
    Why it matters: the court's ruling is starting to lead to real sanctions, not just words.
    Sources: GOV.UK / LBC / Yahoo News / Daily Sun / House of Commons Library / YouTube (TRT World)
  3. 3
    DR Congo: fighting in an Ebola-hit area traps WHO staff inside a hospitalLittle covered in Japan
    • The head of the World Health Organization (WHO), Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, said on the 22nd that heavy fighting since the 21st near a hospital in Ituri province in the east had put patients, families and health workers “at grave risk.” WHO staff sheltered in a maternity ward for more than 24 hours. The hospital is close to where the Ebola outbreak first emerged.
    • According to the WHO, there had been 11,613 cases and 5,678 deaths in DR Congo as of November 17.
    Why it matters: fighting is stopping the work of containing a deadly disease.
    Sources: Al Jazeera / Yahoo News (AFP) / Sight Magazine (Reuters)
  4. 4
    Sudan: drones that hit the capital “were launched from Ethiopia,” an army source saysLittle covered in Japan
    • Early on the 22nd, drones hit homes and a UN building in the capital, Khartoum, killing at least four people, most of them reported to be children. A Sudanese army source said the drones were launched from Bahir Dar airport in Ethiopia, but this has not been verified.
    • No group has claimed the strikes, and the UN has not said who was responsible. Saudi Arabia and Egypt condemned the attacks.
    Why it matters: it raises the possibility that a neighbouring country is being drawn into Sudan's civil war.
    Sources: The New Arab / Arab News Japan / Channels TV / Manila Times (AFP) / anews / YouTube (Middle East Eye) / YouTube (TRT World)

    Poland says Russia twice tried to kill a Ukrainian-American rapper; Denmark expels a Russian diplomatLittle covered in Japan
    • A spokesman for Poland's foreign ministry confirmed an alleged plot by Russian intelligence to kill the Ukrainian-American rapper known as Kyivstoner in Poland. The suspect, allegedly recruited by Moscow, was detained on August 7.
    • On the 20th Denmark announced it was expelling a Russian diplomat, accusing Russian intelligence of plotting to kill the same man at a concert near Copenhagen in September. Danish police also stopped a separate attempt on September 25, arresting three men.
    • Denmark is pushing for EU-wide limits on travel by Russian nationals, and Poland says it fully supports this. It has not been made public whether the same people were behind both plots.
    Why it matters: the war is reaching into everyday life in European cities far from the front line.
    Sources: Al Jazeera / EU Today / Finwire

Elsewhere in the world stories rarely seen in Japanese media

Indonesia: at least 24 people confirmed dead in the candy factory fire
Police said on the 23rd that at least 24 people died in the fire that broke out on the 21st at a candy factory in Serang, Banten province, on the island of Java (an Al Jazeera English video gives the figure as 26). About 30 people are still missing and the search continues. About 300 people were in the complex at the time; 29 were rescued, five of whom were taken to hospital. A presidential adviser said a short circuit in the air-conditioning system may have been the cause, but that further investigation is needed.
Sources: Al Jazeera / Gulf News / Yahoo News / YouTube (Al Jazeera English)
Iowa, US: the Army plans to detonate about 5 tons of a lead-based explosive; a court has paused it and the president has told the Army to wait
The US Army plans to dispose of about 10,000 pounds (roughly 4.5 tonnes) of degraded lead azide, an explosive used in munitions, by detonating it at an ammunition plant in Middletown, Iowa. After environmental groups sued, a federal appeals court temporarily halted the plan. Residents worry about lead spreading into the air and soil. President Trump said on the 23rd that he had ordered the military not to go ahead until the court agrees. The Environmental Protection Agency says the compound has become dangerously unstable and that moving it is risky.
Sources: Al Jazeera / CNN / KCRG / CBS News
UK: a trial of AI-assisted breast cancer screening begins with about 700,000 women
England's National Health Service (NHS) has launched a trial of AI-assisted breast cancer screening. Nearly 700,000 women are expected to take part over three years at 30 sites (some reports say about 660,000). At present two specialists review each image; the trial will test whether one is enough with the help of AI. Women attending routine screening will be told about the trial and can opt out. Some independent experts warn of the risk of overdiagnosing mild disease that would not need treatment.
Sources: Semafor / Manila Times (AFP) / YouTube (TRT World)
Australia: a bill placing a “duty of care” on social media and AI chatbots is before the Senate
Australia's government is advancing a bill (the Digital Duty of Care bill) that would require online services such as social media, games and AI chatbots to meet minimum standards to protect users from harmful material. Services would have to act against violent extremist material, child abuse and fake nude images, and shield children from content that encourages eating disorders or promotes hostility towards women. Social media users would be able to choose whether their feed uses algorithmic recommendations. The bill passed the lower house on October 28 and was debated in the Senate on the 23rd. Opposition senators say the categories of harm are too loosely defined.
Sources: Australian Government / Lexology / Sarah Henderson (Senate speech) / YouTube (TRT World)

Japan top 5 little covered by the big networks, but worth knowing

  1. 1
    A worker dies in an accident at the Kashiwazaki-Kariwa nuclear plant; TEPCO halts all construction work
    At about 11:30 a.m. on the 22nd, at TEPCO's Kashiwazaki-Kariwa nuclear power plant in Niigata Prefecture, more than a dozen glass panels (about 70 kg each) for an office building under construction toppled while being unpacked, and a 38-year-old man employed by a subcontractor was crushed and died. TEPCO said it would halt all construction work at the plant until safety is confirmed. It is reported to be the first fatal accident at the plant since 1997, and there have been 15 accidents involving injuries this fiscal year. Why it is worth knowing: it raises questions about on-site safety at a company that operates nuclear plants.
    Sources: NHK / BSN Niigata (Yahoo! News) / Jiji Press (Infoseek)
  2. 2
    Aichi survey: 39.7% say they understand “multicultural coexistence,” short of four in ten
    In a survey of residents by Aichi Prefecture, 39.7% said they understood the idea of a “multicultural society” (7.5% “well” and 32.2% “to some extent”), while 40.1% said they did not. Asked about many foreign residents living in the prefecture, respondents cited worries about “trouble arising from differences in customs and culture” (39%) and “worsening public safety” (34.2%). The survey was sent to 3,000 people in July and 1,701 replied. Aichi has the third-largest number of foreign residents in Japan. The prefecture says it will step up its efforts to inform people. Why it is worth knowing: how neighbours get to know one another matters in every town.
    Sources: NHK / Chunichi Shimbun / Higashi Aichi Shimbun
  3. 3
    Bird flu confirmed at a poultry farm in Toyohashi, Aichi; about 80,000 hens to be culled
    Aichi Prefecture said on the 23rd that highly pathogenic bird flu had been confirmed at an egg farm in Toyohashi, and it began culling about 80,000 hens. The farm had alerted the prefecture on the 22nd after more birds than usual died. The prefecture restricted the movement of birds and eggs at 35 farms, with about 2.81 million birds, within 10 km of the farm. The work is due to be completed by the 27th. It is the third case confirmed in Japan this season. Why it is worth knowing: it affects egg prices and the livelihoods of poultry farmers.
    Sources: Japan Agricultural News / Chunichi Shimbun / Yomiuri (Yahoo! News) / Higashi Aichi Shimbun
  4. 4
    iDeCo (individual pension plans) changes from December 1: people can join until they turn 70
    iDeCo, a scheme in which people save for their own retirement, changes from December 1. The age limit for joining rises from under 65 in principle to under 70. Monthly contribution limits also rise: from 68,000 yen to 75,000 yen for the self-employed and others, and to 62,000 yen for employees without a company pension (the higher limits are said to apply from contributions for January 2027). The upper age for starting to receive benefits (75) does not change. Why it is worth knowing: it widens the choices for preparing for retirement. Please check the details with a financial institution.
    Sources: Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare / Government Public Relations Online / Resona Bank
  5. 5
    Minimum wage: the last three prefectures raise theirs in early December; the national average becomes 1,177 yen (original source not confirmed)
    This fiscal year's new minimum wages take effect in Iwate and Kumamoto on December 1 and in Okinawa on December 2, completing the rise in all 47 prefectures. The national average is 1,177 yen an hour, up 56 yen (about 5%) from the previous year. The highest is Tokyo at 1,280 yen and the lowest is Miyazaki at 1,085 yen. The figures come from summaries on explanatory websites; we could not check the government announcement itself. Why it is worth knowing: it affects the hourly pay of part-time workers and the running of small businesses and shops.
    Sources: Tax & Labor Navi / Hojyokin Portal / hiro-money

Food and health from Dr Toshiaki Yoshino's videos

Dr Yoshino: linoleic acid, found in vegetable oils, takes a long time to leave the body
In a video posted on November 22, Dr Toshiaki Yoshino says the following. Linoleic acid is found in vegetable oils and is an oil the body cannot do without, but Japanese people eat far too much of it — in his words, “50 to 60 times the acceptable amount.” He says it takes 640 days for linoleic acid stored in body fat to fall by half, and about 3,000 days to fall to 5%, citing a paper from 1964.
These are Dr Yoshino's own views (the figures are those he gives in the video; we have not checked them). Please talk to your own doctor before making decisions about your health or diet.
Sources: YouTube (吉野敏明チャンネル)

Today's Mindfulness Let's Practice Mindfulness

Three minutes with a hand on your chest, after news that hurts

This morning brought news of people attacked while going to help the wounded, and of a man killed while bringing water to others. Reading it, you may have felt your chest tighten. That is because there is care for others inside you.
For just three minutes, gently hold yourself, together with that feeling.

  1. Sit in a comfortable position. Lift your shoulders once, then let them drop.
  2. Rest the palm of one hand softly on the centre of your chest. Feel the warmth and the weight of your hand.
  3. As you breathe in, your chest rises; as you breathe out, it sinks. Feel that movement under your palm for five breaths.
  4. If sadness or anger comes up, say silently, "My chest hurts right now." Do not try to push it away; simply keep the warmth of your hand there.
  5. Finally, say slowly and silently: "May the people dear to me be safe. May the people of this country be safe. May the people of the world be safe. May all living beings be safe." Lower your hand, and finish.

A heart that hurts is a sign of kindness. Whenever it gets hard, come back to the warmth of your palm.
