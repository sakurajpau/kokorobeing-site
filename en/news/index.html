  2. 2
    Strait of Hormuz: Iran says “a tanker touched a mine and exploded”; “until our seven conditions are met, ships without permission will not pass”
    • Iran's Revolutionary Guards announced that a tanker trying to cross the Strait of Hormuz by a “route that is not permitted” touched a mine (a bomb placed in the sea), exploded and caught fire. The place is the sea north of Oman, and some reports say two ships were hit. This does not always match the counts of the agencies that watch over safety at sea.
    • A commander of Iran's military said the strait is closed to ships without permission, and will stay closed until the seven conditions Iran has set out are met. The Italian newspaper Il Sole 24 Ore reports that only two ships passed in the last 24 hours, and 400 ships are waiting. On the other hand, TRT World reports, using data from a company that tracks ship movements, that the cargo passing through is 40–42% less than before the war, so the numbers differ depending on how they are counted.
    • The Revolutionary Guards also announced that near Hengam Island they fired warning shots and made a US F-16 fighter jet change direction. No reaction from the US side has been reported.
    Why it matters: this is the sea that much of the crude oil coming to Japan has passed through. The longer the days when ships cannot pass go on, the more it affects the price of gasoline and electricity.

World top 5 important stories that get little airtime on Japanese TV

  1. 1
    DR Congo: fighting grows heavy again in the east; more than 320,000 people leave their homes in one weekLittle covered in Japan
    • In South Kivu province, in the east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo in central Africa, fighting between the government army and the armed group FRN (earlier called M23 and other names, and now led by former President Kabila) is growing heavy again.     • Sri Lanka's government has put before parliament a new bill to replace the 1979 “Prevention of Terrorism Act,” and a bill to prevent organized crime.     Indonesia: river water pours into a gold mine run without permission; 16 people have died and 10 are still trapped
    • In Bogor in the west of Indonesia's Java island, late at night on the 23rd, a river overflowed in heavy rain, and water poured into the shafts of a gold mine that had no permit. According to the AP news agency, 16 bodies have been found, and 10 people are thought to be still inside (the numbers differ a little from report to report).

  5. 5
    The climate conference COP31 ends without its most important agreement; the words “fossil fuels” disappeared from the document
    • The UN climate conference (COP31) held in Antalya, Turkey, ended on November 22, two days later than planned, but the summary document, the “Antalya Accord,” was not adopted. It became the longest UN climate conference ever.
    • The first draft had words about phasing out fossil fuels such as oil and coal, but because of opposition from oil-producing countries, they disappeared from the second draft. The EU, the UK and the countries most easily harmed by climate change did not accept it, saying it was “too weak.” The target for money to prepare for harm (120 billion dollars) was also dropped from the draft.
    • On the other hand, more than 20 countries put forward plans to phase out fossil fuels, and about 80 countries signed a pledge to spread efforts to run daily life on electricity. The agreement made in Dubai in 2023 to “move away from fossil fuels” still stands as it is. The next conference is in November 2027 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.
    • On the 27th, Middle East Eye put out a video comparing this conference to a quiz show, and reported harshly that there is “no new money, and only a promise to try again next year.”
    Why it matters: harm from heavy rain and heat is increasing, yet it is becoming harder for the world to move in step.
    Sources: Carbon Brief / ABC News (AP) / Green Central Banking / Climate Action / YouTube (Middle East Eye)

Elsewhere in the world stories rarely seen in Japanese media

Peru: heavy El Niño rain floods the northern city of Piura again
In Piura in northern Peru, in South America, streets, markets and squares were flooded by heavy rain on the 26th. The same happened in nearby Sullana, Talara, Tambogrande and other places. The weather office recorded 59.2 mm of rain at its Piura station, and says there is a risk that heavy rain will continue until the 29th. Food and health from Dr Toshiaki Yoshino's videos

Dr Yoshino: “When you eat breakfast and chew it well, your bowels start to move”
In his video of November 27, Dr Toshiaki Yoshino talks about constipation and morning habits. Dr Yoshino said that in the days when he was going to academic meetings abroad, he saw many times how a long line formed in front of the toilets after breakfast was served on the plane. The body clocks of the people on board are all different, so why do they want to go at about the same time? Dr Yoshino's explanation is that there is a mechanism in which the large intestine starts to move when food enters the stomach (the gastrocolic reflex). Dr Yoshino says this mechanism is also why babies poo after drinking milk. According to Dr Yoshino, in adults this mechanism works strongly in the morning, and it happens more easily when “it getting light,” “chewing well,” “swallowing” and “the stomach filling up” all come together. For this reason, Dr Yoshino recommends not skipping breakfast or making do with only coffee or a jelly drink, and eating while looking at the food and chewing it well, not while watching TV or videos. He also said to get dietary fiber from foods such as mushrooms, seaweed and wild mountain vegetables (increasing it suddenly puts a strain on the stomach, so do it little by little, he says). Dr Yoshino also explained the differences between men's and women's bodies from the way people lived long, long ago, but it seems best to hear this as Dr Yoshino's own idea.
These are Dr Yoshino's own views (we have not checked the explanations of how the body works; this summary is based on the video's automatic captions). Please talk to your own doctor before deciding anything about your body.
Sources: YouTube (Dr Toshiaki Yoshino's channel)

A heartwarming story one gentle story to end with

Malawi: an AI classroom that “answers when you speak to it,” for children who have trouble seeing; it was made by a 25-year-old man
In Malawi in southeastern Africa, a learning system called “Blind Classroom,” for children who cannot see or have trouble seeing, is spreading. It reads the words of textbooks and other materials aloud, and when a child asks a question by voice, it answers. It has only three buttons, and can be used even without the internet. It was made by Staff Nyoni, a 25-year-old software developer. He says two things had always stayed in his heart: his father, who has trouble seeing, struggled to read, and a girl in his neighborhood who had trouble seeing gave up school. It is now used at 14 schools in the country, and the plan is to spread it to 20 more schools next year. Faith, whose dream is to become a doctor, says, “Without this technology, I could not have believed I could become a doctor. I was able to have hope once again.” Another student said, “I can ask again as many times as I need until I understand. I do not have to wait for someone to read to me.”
Sources: YouTube (TRT World) / D+C (Development and Cooperation) / Broadcast Media Africa

Today's Mindfulness Let's Practice Mindfulness

Three minutes of counting your breaths, for when you feel anxious about what lies ahead

This morning brought the view that "two wars are becoming one," news of ships that cannot pass through a strait, and the story of villagers who were told to leave their homes in ten minutes. Reading them, you may have felt your worry grow: "What will happen to the world from here?" We worry about the future because there are things we want to protect.
For just three minutes, let's come back to the breath, here and now.

  1. Sit in a comfortable position. Close your eyes, or let them rest softly on a spot below you. Let your shoulders relax.
  2. Feel the breath coming in and going out through your nose, just as it is. You do not need to change its length or depth.
  3. Each time you breathe out, count in your mind: "one," "two," … When you reach "ten," go back to "one."
  4. If the news or a worry comes up and you lose count, that is fine. Notice, "I was thinking," and start again from "one."
  5. Finally, take one slow, deep breath and feel your feet touching the floor. Gently open your eyes, and finish.

No one knows what lies ahead. But this one breath, right now, is surely here. With each breath you counted, your mind came home to "now."

Why?
Anxiety tends to grow when the mind keeps replaying "what might happen," things that have not happened yet. Turning your attention to something simple and certain, like counting breaths, gives the mind a foothold for coming back to "now." Even when you lose count, noticing and returning is the practice itself, so you do not have to do it well. That, too, suits an anxious moment.
