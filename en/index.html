ABOUT

What is KoKoRoBeing?

The meaning behind the name

KoKoRo means “mind” in Japanese. Being means simply being who you are.

We often try to prove our worth by doing: working harder, doing things properly, being more useful. As we keep running, we slowly lose touch with how we actually feel.

KoKoRoBeing is a place to let go of “doing” for a while and return to simply “being.” It is fine to waver. It is fine to feel out of balance. We hope to be a place where you can accept those waves and come back to yourself, again and again.

Connecting in a home for the mind

You talk with others and feel relieved: “It wasn’t just me.” You breathe together, and each person’s breath seems to become one. I have seen moments like these many times.

It was mindfulness, and the friends I met through it, that helped me learn I could trust people.

KoKoRoBeing is a place where we connect, then each return to our own daily lives. Not to stay here forever, but to go back to our lives again.

Who this place is for

  • People who tend to try too hard at work or in relationships
  • People who have felt stifled by lines like “right or wrong” and “working hard or being lazy”
  • People who want to feel like themselves again while caring for the connection between mind and body
Three things we value
Being with you, rather than chasing results
We don’t brush things aside with quick advice. We stay quietly with what is happening now.
No exaggeration, no pressure
We don’t promise dramatic effects or use words that make you anxious.
It’s okay to waver
You don’t have to be perfect. We keep practising coming back, even while wavering.
MINDFULNESS

What is mindfulness?

Mindfulness is often described as “paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and non-judgmentally.” This description comes from Dr. Jon Kabat-Zinn, who brought mindfulness into medical settings in the United States.

Without noticing, our minds often run on “autopilot.” We replay conversations that are already over, or worry about things that haven’t happened yet. Our body is here, but our mind has gone off to the past or the future.

Mindfulness is the practice of noticing that autopilot and coming back to “here and now.” You don’t need special tools, a religion, or any experience. You can start with a single breath.

What research shows

A 2014 review of 47 studies (3,515 participants) found that mindfulness meditation programs had a small to moderate effect in easing anxiety, low mood and pain (moderate strength of evidence).

They were not found to be better than medication or other psychological therapies. At KoKoRoBeing, we share mindfulness not as a “treatment,” but as a “habit” that makes daily life a little easier.

Source: Goyal M, et al. Meditation Programs for Psychological Stress and Well-being. JAMA Internal Medicine. 2014;174(3):357-368.

How we practise at KoKoRoBeing

BREATH
Return to the breath
Start by noticing each in-breath and out-breath. A place you can return to anytime, anywhere.
BODY
Notice the body
Through sensations in places like your shoulders or the soles of your feet, you notice the state of your mind (a somatic approach).
WRITING
Write to settle
Write down what you felt in a few short lines to sort out your thoughts (journaling).
DAILY
Practise in daily life
Walking, eating, talking. Not in special moments, but in everyday life, we practise coming back to “now.”

PROFILE

Founder: Asami Sakura (咲空麻美)

A mindfulness instructor, counsellor and coach based in Australia. I have been living between Japan and Australia for 17 years. Since 2019, I have offered online sessions and courses, working with more than 750 participants in total. I am currently writing a book.

My starting point is my father, who struggled with alcohol dependence. As a child, I was told that I was the reason he drank. I began studying the mind because I wanted to understand why my kind father had become that way. Looking back, I think alcohol was his “home for the mind.” Everyone around him kept saying “stop.” So did I. But maybe what he was really waiting for was someone to say, “I understand why you want to drink.”

After university, I learned programming at a software company, and later worked in administration and accounting. I have had many jobs, including quality control, secretary, tax office, factory work, journalist, tour guide and nature photographer.

I worked hard and seriously in every job, but inside I kept drawing lines between “right and wrong” and “working hard and being lazy,” and I felt stifled. My wish to leave Japan grew stronger. After a year in Canada, I moved to Okinawa, and then to Australia. While living between two cultures, I encountered mindfulness, and little by little I regained the feeling of coming back to “now” as I am.

In Australia, I also accompanied junior and senior high school students on short study-abroad trips from Japan. Each time, there was a student who had not been able to go into their classroom in Japan. In a place where they were not judged and were given time until they were ready, their eyes slowly began to shine. At the end of two weeks, they said goodbye to their host families in tears. They taught me that people need a “home for the mind.”

From Masaki Yoshida, president of the Mindfulness Meditation Association in Japan, I learned that the parts of myself I had rejected are also me, and that every emotion deserves to be cared for.

At Maji-bun Academy, led by Yasumasa Maeda, a former copy editor at the Asahi Shimbun, I spent a year learning how to structure writing. That is where I learned what it feels like to entrust a formless mind to words.

Today, combining hypnotherapy, work with the subconscious, somatic (body-based) approaches and focusing, I run KoKoRoBeing as a place where people who try too hard can come back to themselves.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is mindfulness?

It is the practice of paying attention to your breath and body sensations “in this moment,” without judging them as good or bad. No special tools or experience are needed.

What happens in the free trial session?

For about 20 minutes, we listen to how you are and what is on your mind, and settle the breath together. We can also tell you about individual sessions and the 3-month program.

Can men join?

Yes. Anyone is welcome, regardless of gender or age.

Do I have to turn my camera on?

We’d love to see you on camera, but camera off is fine too. Please put your own comfort first.

I’m not comfortable speaking out loud.

You can talk using your microphone or type in the chat, whichever is easier for you.

Can I cancel or reschedule the free trial?

Yes. Please let us know by 8 pm Japan time, two days before your session.
